# Editorial decisions

Use the requested voice, relevant `VOICE.md`, and author samples ahead of generic
preferences. Make an edit because it solves a problem in this passage, not because
its sentence length, punctuation, or structure matches a pattern.

## Purpose and density

Ask what each passage gives the reader. An explanation, example, condition,
transition, joke, or personal aside can earn its place. Cut repetition when it adds
nothing; keep a reminder or deliberate refrain when it helps the reader follow.

Let length follow the work the passage needs to do. A long introduction can establish
necessary context; a short one can get directly to a question. Split a paragraph
when its ideas become hard to follow, not after a fixed number of sentences.

## Clarity and specificity

Use concrete details from the material when an abstract statement leaves the reader
unsure what happened or why it matters. Ask for missing examples or mark the gap.
A general claim cannot become a precise number without evidence.

Name the actor when that clarifies responsibility and the source identifies one.
Passive voice is useful when the actor is unknown or the action matters more.
Preserve technical terms the intended reader needs. Explain unfamiliar ideas at
the first point where the reader must understand them.

## Rhythm and formatting

Read for accidental repetition and places where a reader must backtrack. Vary
rhythm only where it sounds mechanical; keep the author's natural cadence,
fragments, and long sentences when they work.

Use headings, lists, tables, emphasis, and code when they make this material easier
to understand or find. Plain paragraphs may be the best form. For a tutorial or
reference-oriented post, check whether someone scanning can locate what they need.
A personal essay does not need to behave like a reference page.

## Titles, openings, and endings

Keep an existing title if it honestly describes the post. Offer alternatives when
requested or when a real choice about meaning or emphasis would help the author.
Explain that choice without a fixed number of options.

The opening should give this reader a reason to continue: a question, useful
context, an observation, or a scene can do that. It need not promise a revelation.
The ending should fit what the body establishes. A result, limitation, unresolved
question, or quiet stopping point can work without a summary or call to action.

## When the piece is not working

- **Too broad:** identify competing purposes and propose a narrower scope. Use
  `create-drafted-blog-post` if the author wants to rethink the argument or order.
- **Too abstract:** find the point where an example or mechanism would help, and
  use available material rather than inventing a story.
- **Weakly supported:** find evidence, preserve limits, or ask to reduce the claim.
- **Off-voice:** compare with relevant author samples before changing tone. Ask
  about a specific unresolved choice rather than requesting a new voice interview.
- **Too long or short:** identify repetition or an unanswered reader question.
  Change length only to solve that problem; do not pad or cut to meet a formula.
